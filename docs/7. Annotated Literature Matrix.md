# Emotional Contagion

## Foundational Source

Hatfield, E., Cacioppo, J. T., & Rapson, R. L. (1994). *Emotional contagion*. Cambridge University Press.

## Core Argument

Emotions spread through social interaction via unconscious imitation, synchronization, and feedback processes.

## Major Findings

- Emotional states transfer between individuals.
- Negative emotions often spread rapidly.
- Group emotional climates emerge through repeated interaction.
- Emotions can influence behavior without conscious awareness.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Provides the foundational mechanism explaining how emotional temperature spreads from one actor to a larger group.

# Toxic Workplace Behavior

## Foundational Source

Sutton, R. I. (2007). *The no asshole rule: Building a civilized workplace and surviving one that isn't*. Warner Business Books.

## Core Argument

A single persistently negative individual can cause disproportionate organizational harm. While organizations often focus on productivity, expertise, or status, they frequently underestimate the collective costs associated with toxic behavior. Sutton argues that harmful actors affect not only direct targets but also witnesses, bystanders, and the broader organizational climate.

## Major Findings

- Toxic actors reduce morale.
- Turnover increases.
- Organizational performance declines.
- Witnesses experience stress and reduced engagement.
- Harm extends beyond direct targets.
- Organizational cultures may normalize harmful behavior when high-status individuals are protected.

## Higher Education Applications

The relevance of Sutton's work becomes particularly visible in colleges and universities.

Examples may include:

- Faculty whose behavior discourages participation during meetings.
- Senior scholars who normalize disrespect toward colleagues or students.
- Department members who use intimidation, exclusion, or public criticism as informal forms of control.
- Influential individuals whose conduct is tolerated because of status, funding, publications, or institutional power.
- Academic environments where students and junior faculty learn to remain silent rather than challenge harmful behavior.

A second and often overlooked phenomenon is the "missing person" dynamic.

These individuals are frequently:

- Absent from difficult conversations.
- Minimally involved in departmental service.
- Unavailable during periods of organizational stress.
- Detached from the everyday experiences of colleagues and students.

Yet they may continue to accumulate rewards, promotions, recognition, or institutional status.

Unlike the overtly toxic actor, the missing person does not increase emotional temperature through conflict. Instead, their absence may create perceptions of inequity, resentment, and organizational cynicism among those carrying disproportionate workloads.

Over time, members begin asking:

- Who is doing the work?
- Who receives the rewards?
- Whose contributions are visible?
- Whose absences go unnoticed?

These questions influence trust, morale, and organizational legitimacy.

From a group ecology perspective, the absence itself becomes socially meaningful.

The problem is not merely that the individual is unavailable. The problem is that the environment communicates that participation and contribution are optional while rewards remain available.

This dynamic may function as a form of passive climate deterioration, contributing to withdrawal, disengagement, and perceptions of organizational unfairness.
## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Sutton's work provides empirical support for the proposition that a single actor can function as a climate-changing catalyst.

The significance of the actor lies not simply in personality, but in the capacity to alter the environment surrounding them.

# Workplace Incivility

## Foundational Sources

Pearson, C. M., Andersson, L. M., & Porath, C. L. (2000). Assessing and attacking workplace incivility. *Organizational Dynamics, 29*(2), 123-137.

Porath, C., & Pearson, C. (2013). The price of incivility. *Harvard Business Review, 91*(1-2), 114-121.

## Core Argument

Low-intensity deviant behavior contributes to widespread organizational dysfunction.

## Major Findings

- Witnesses experience negative effects.
- Productivity declines.
- Cooperation decreases.
- Incivility becomes contagious.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Demonstrates how small negative interactions produce large ecological effects.

---

# Psychological Safety

## Foundational Source

Edmondson, A. C. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 44*(2), 350-383.

## Core Argument

Individuals contribute more fully when interpersonal risk is low.

## Major Findings

- Psychological safety predicts participation.
- Innovation improves in psychologically safe groups.
- Fear suppresses communication.
- Trust promotes learning.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Provides measurable indicators of emotional temperature and climate health.

---

# Organizational Climate

## Foundational Sources

Schneider, B. (1975). Organizational climates: An essay. *Personnel Psychology, 28*(4), 447-479.

Schneider, B., Ehrhart, M. G., & Macey, W. H. (2013). Organizational climate and culture. *Annual Review of Psychology, 64*, 361-388.

## Core Argument

Shared perceptions of organizational practices create climate.

## Major Findings

- Climate influences behavior.
- Climate predicts organizational outcomes.
- Employee perceptions affect performance.
- Culture and climate influence one another.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Provides the framework for understanding group climate as a collective phenomenon.

---

# Social Networks

## Foundational Sources

Granovetter, M. S. (1973). The strength of weak ties. *American Journal of Sociology, 78*(6), 1360-1380.

Granovetter, M. S. (1985). Economic action and social structure: The problem of embeddedness. *American Journal of Sociology, 91*(3), 481-510.

## Core Argument

Social relationships function as channels for information, influence, and behavioral diffusion.

## Major Findings

- Influence spreads through networks.
- Position matters.
- Network structure affects outcomes.
- Weak ties facilitate transmission across groups.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Provides the network foundation for understanding positive and negative nodes.

# Symbolic Interactionism

## Foundational Sources

Mead, G. H. (1934). *Mind, self, and society*. University of Chicago Press.

Blumer, H. (1969). *Symbolic interactionism: Perspective and method*. University of California Press.

Goffman, E. (1959). *The presentation of self in everyday life*. Doubleday.

## Core Argument

People act based on meanings created and negotiated through interaction.

## Major Findings

- Meanings emerge socially.
- Identity develops interactionally.
- Social reality is constructed.
- Group norms are continuously negotiated.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Explains why the same behavior can produce different effects across different environments.

# Positive Organizational Scholarship

## Foundational Sources

Cameron, K. S., Dutton, J. E., & Quinn, R. E. (Eds.). (2003). *Positive organizational scholarship: Foundations of a new discipline*. Berrett-Koehler.

Cameron, K. S. (2012). *Positive leadership: Strategies for extraordinary performance*. Berrett-Koehler.

## Core Argument

Organizations flourish when positive practices, relationships, and virtues are cultivated.

## Major Findings

- Positive climates enhance performance.
- Virtuous behaviors spread.
- Positive relationships build resilience.
- Organizational health is measurable.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Provides the foundation for Positive Nodes and climate restoration.

---

# Organizational Resilience

## Foundational Sources

Weick, K. E., & Sutcliffe, K. M. (2007). *Managing the unexpected: Resilient performance in an age of uncertainty* (2nd ed.). Jossey-Bass.

Weick, K. E. (1995). *Sensemaking in organizations*. Sage.

## Core Argument

Resilient organizations adapt, learn, and recover from disruption.

## Major Findings

- Recovery is an organizational process.
- Resilience depends on collective awareness.
- Systems can absorb shocks.
- Sensemaking influences adaptation.

## Relevance to Catalytic Node Theory

Explains why some groups resist contagion and recover following climate disruption.

1. Individual Actors

## Preliminary Proposition

> The influence of a catalytic node is moderated by environmental conditions, transmitted through social networks, interpreted through symbolic interaction, reflected in emotional temperature, and ultimately expressed through organizational climate.
